# Chapter 7 — Producers in the Short Run

The main things Chapter 7 wants you to know are firm organization/financing, accounting vs. economic profit, short-run production, diminishing marginal returns, and short-run costs. 

## 7.1 What Are Firms?

### Forms of business organization

| Type | Main idea |
|---|---|
| Single proprietorship | One owner-manager; personally responsible for debts |
| Ordinary partnership | 2+ owners; partners personally responsible for debts |
| Limited partnership | General partners run firm and have full liability; limited partners have liability limited to investment |
| Corporation | Separate legal identity; owners generally not personally responsible for firm's actions |
| State-owned enterprise | Government owned; called a **Crown corporation** in Canada |
| Non-profit organization | Provides goods/services; profits stay in organization |

    

### Multinational enterprise

A firm operating in more than one country is a:

\[
\boxed{\text{Multinational Enterprise (MNE)}}
\]

---

## Financing Firms

### Financial capital vs. physical capital

**Financial capital** = money raised to finance the business.

**Physical capital** = productive assets such as machinery, factories, offices, and vehicles. 

The two main sources of financial capital are:

\[
\boxed{\text{Equity and Debt}}
\]

### Equity

Equity means **ownership**.

Corporations raise equity by selling:

- shares
- stocks
- equities

Profits paid to shareholders are:

\[
\boxed{\text{Dividends}}
\]

### Debt

Debt means the lender is a **creditor, not an owner**.

The firm must repay:

\[
\boxed{\text{Principal + Interest}}
\]

Examples include bank loans and bonds. 

---

# 7.2 Production, Costs, and Profits

## Production Function

The production function shows the **maximum output** obtainable from a given combination of inputs:

\[
\boxed{Q=f(L,K)}
\]

where:

- \(Q\) = output
- \(L\) = labour
- \(K\) = capital

---

# Accounting Profit vs. Economic Profit

This is very important.

### Explicit costs

Costs involving resources used by the firm, such as:

- wages
- rent
- interest
- intermediate inputs
- depreciation

Accounting profit:

\[
\boxed{\text{Accounting Profit}
=\text{Revenue}-\text{Explicit Costs}}
\]

### Implicit costs

Implicit costs do **not necessarily involve a market transaction**, but they have an opportunity cost.

Examples:

- owner's time
- owner's invested capital

### Economic profit

\[
\boxed{
\text{Economic Profit}
=
\text{Revenue}
-
(\text{Explicit Costs}+\text{Implicit Costs})
}
\]

or:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Economic Profit}
=
\text{Accounting Profit}-\text{Implicit Costs}
}
\]

### Important relationship

Because economic profit subtracts **more costs**:

\[
\boxed{\text{Economic Profit}\leq\text{Accounting Profit}}
\]

Example from the slides:

Accounting profit:

\[
840
\]

Implicit costs:

\[
265
\]

Economic profit:

\[
840-265=\boxed{575}
\]

---

# Profit Formula

When the chapter refers to a firm's profit, it means **economic profit**:

\[
\boxed{\pi=TR-TC}
\]

where:

- \(\pi\) = economic profit
- \(TR\) = total revenue
- \(TC\) = total economic cost

---

# Short Run vs. Long Run vs. Very Long Run

| Period | What can change? |
|---|---|
| Short run | At least one factor is fixed |
| Long run | All factors can vary; technology fixed |
| Very long run | Inputs **and technology** can vary |

The terms do **not** refer to a specific number of months or years.  

---

# 7.3 Production in the Short Run

## Total Product

\[
\boxed{TP=\text{total output produced}}
\]

## Average Product

\[
\boxed{AP=\frac{TP}{L}}
\]

where \(L\) is labour.

## Marginal Product

Marginal product measures the additional output produced by an additional unit of labour:

\[
\boxed{MP=\frac{\Delta TP}{\Delta L}}
\]

 

### Example

Suppose:

\[
L: 5\rightarrow6
\]

and:

\[
TP:35\rightarrow55
\]

Then:

\[
MP=\frac{55-35}{6-5}=20
\]

So the sixth worker adds:

\[
\boxed{20\text{ units}}
\]

---

## Total Product Graph

The slide's production table shows TP increasing from 0 to 120 as labour rises from 0 to 13 workers. Initially TP rises at an increasing rate, then eventually rises at a decreasing rate. 

---

# Law of Diminishing Marginal Returns

This is one of the biggest Chapter 7 concepts.

In the short run:

- at least one factor is fixed
- more units of a variable factor are added
- eventually each additional unit adds **less output than the previous one**

\[
\boxed{\text{Eventually }MP\downarrow}
\]

Why?

Each new worker has less of the fixed factor—such as machinery or workspace—to work with. 

### Important

Diminishing marginal returns does **not** mean total output immediately falls.

It means:

\[
\boxed{\text{TP can still rise, but at a decreasing rate}}
\]

---

# Relationship Between MP and AP

This relationship is extremely testable.

\[
\boxed{MP>AP\Rightarrow AP\uparrow}
\]

\[
\boxed{MP<AP\Rightarrow AP\downarrow}
\]

and:

\[
\boxed{MP=AP\text{ at maximum AP}}
\]

The MP curve crosses AP exactly where AP reaches its maximum. 

### Memory trick

Think of your grade average.

If your next grade is **above your average**, your average rises.

If your next grade is **below your average**, your average falls.

Marginal product works the same way.

## AP and MP Graph

Notice that MP reaches its peak first and then begins falling. AP keeps rising until MP crosses it.

---

# 7.4 Short-Run Costs

The basic cost relationship is:

\[
\boxed{TC=TFC+TVC}
\]

where:

- \(TC\) = total cost
- \(TFC\) = total fixed cost
- \(TVC\) = total variable cost

Average total cost:

\[
\boxed{ATC=AFC+AVC}
\]

Useful formulas:

\[
\boxed{AFC=\frac{TFC}{Q}}
\]

\[
\boxed{AVC=\frac{TVC}{Q}}
\]

\[
\boxed{ATC=\frac{TC}{Q}}
\]

---

# Marginal Cost

Marginal cost is the increase in total cost caused by producing additional output:

\[
\boxed{MC=\frac{\Delta TC}{\Delta Q}}
\]

Because fixed costs do not change as output changes:

\[
\boxed{\Delta TC=\Delta TVC}
\]

so marginal cost comes from changes in variable cost. 

---

# Short-Run Cost Curve Relationships

Key rules:

\[
\boxed{MC<ATC\Rightarrow ATC\downarrow}
\]

\[
\boxed{MC>ATC\Rightarrow ATC\uparrow}
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{MC=ATC\text{ at minimum ATC}}
\]

The same relationship exists with AVC:

\[
\boxed{MC=AVC\text{ at minimum AVC}}
\]

The slides explicitly note that MC intersects ATC and AVC at their minimum points. 

---

# Why are MC and AVC U-shaped?

The production and cost curves are connected.

When marginal product rises:

\[
MP\uparrow\Rightarrow MC\downarrow
\]

When marginal product falls:

\[
MP\downarrow\Rightarrow MC\uparrow
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{MP\text{ maximum}\Longleftrightarrow MC\text{ minimum}}
\]

Similarly:

\[
\boxed{AP\text{ maximum}\Longleftrightarrow AVC\text{ minimum}}
\]

These inverse production–cost relationships assume labour is the only variable input and its wage is constant.

This is very useful for multiple-choice questions.

---

# AFC Behaviour

Because fixed cost stays constant while output increases:

\[
AFC=\frac{TFC}{Q}
\]

therefore:

\[
\boxed{Q\uparrow\Rightarrow AFC\downarrow}
\]

AFC continually falls as output increases.

It does **not** turn upward.

---

# Capacity

The firm's **capacity** is the output at which short-run average total cost is minimized:

\[
\boxed{\text{Capacity}=\text{output at minimum ATC}}
\]

If a firm produces less than that quantity:

\[
\boxed{\text{Excess capacity}}
\]

---

# Cost Curve Shifts

This is another good exam topic.

### Variable-factor price increases

Example: wage increases.

Then:

\[
\boxed{MC\uparrow}
\]

and:

\[
\boxed{ATC\uparrow}
\]

because producing each additional unit is more expensive. 

### Fixed-factor price increases

Example: higher building rent that does not depend on output.

Then:

\[
\boxed{ATC\uparrow}
\]

but:

\[
\boxed{MC\text{ unchanged}}
\]

because fixed costs don't change when one extra unit is produced. 

### Great cheat-sheet table

| Cost change | ATC | MC |
|---|---:|---:|
| Variable input price ↑ | ↑ | ↑ |
| Fixed input price ↑ | ↑ | **No change** |

---

# Chapter 8 — Producers in the Long Run

Chapter 8 moves from a situation where some inputs are fixed to one where **all inputs can be changed**. The main topics are cost minimization, input substitution, LRAC, economies/diseconomies of scale, the relationship between LRAC and SRATC, and technological change. 

# 8.1 Long Run: No Fixed Factors

Remember:

\[
\boxed{\text{Short run: some inputs fixed}}
\]

\[
\boxed{\text{Long run: all inputs variable}}
\]

In the long run, firms can choose between many different combinations of labour and capital. 

---

# Technical Efficiency

A production method is **technically efficient** if inputs are combined so as to maximize output.

A technique is clearly inefficient if another technique can produce the **same output using less of at least one input and no more of the others**.

For example, the slide compares techniques for producing 1,000 widgets. Technique D uses 30 labour and 60 capital while A uses only 25 labour and 50 capital for the same output, so D is obviously dominated. 

But:

\[
\boxed{\text{Technical efficiency does NOT automatically mean lowest cost}}
\]

The firm must choose the technically efficient method that has the **lowest monetary cost**. 

---

# Cost Minimization

Profit maximization implies the firm should produce any output at the **lowest possible cost**. 

Suppose:

- \(MP_L\) = marginal product of labour
- \(MP_K\) = marginal product of capital
- \(p_L\) = price of labour
- \(p_K\) = price of capital

Cost minimization requires:

\[
\boxed{
\frac{MP_L}{p_L}
=
\frac{MP_K}{p_K}
}
\]

Equivalent form:

\[
\boxed{
\frac{MP_K}{MP_L}
=
\frac{p_K}{p_L}
}
\]

---

# How to Interpret \(MP/\text{Price}\)

Think of:

\[
\boxed{\frac{MP}{P_{\text{input}}}}
\]

as:

> **extra output per dollar spent on that input**

Suppose:

\[
\frac{MP_L}{p_L}=2
\]

and:

\[
\frac{MP_K}{p_K}=5
\]

Capital gives more output per dollar.

Therefore the firm should:

\[
\boxed{\text{use more capital and less labour}}
\]

until the two ratios become equal.

### Rule

If:

\[
\frac{MP_L}{p_L}>
\frac{MP_K}{p_K}
\]

use:

\[
\boxed{\text{more labour, less capital}}
\]

If:

\[
\frac{MP_K}{p_K}>
\frac{MP_L}{p_L}
\]

use:

\[
\boxed{\text{more capital, less labour}}
\]

At cost minimization:

\[
\boxed{\frac{MP_L}{p_L}=\frac{MP_K}{p_K}}
\]

The slides explain that if the ratios are unequal, input substitution can reduce cost. 

---

# Principle of Substitution

If the relative price of an input changes:

\[
\boxed{\text{use relatively more of the cheaper input}}
\]

and:

\[
\boxed{\text{use relatively less of the more expensive input}}
\]

Example:

If wages increase while capital price stays unchanged:

\[
\boxed{\text{Labour becomes relatively expensive}}
\]

So the firm tends to substitute:

\[
\boxed{\text{Capital for labour}}
\]

---

# Long-Run Average Cost

The LRAC curve shows:

\[
\boxed{\text{lowest possible average cost at each output when ALL inputs can vary}}
\]

Since there are no fixed factors in the long run, the short-run distinction among AFC, AVC, and ATC is not needed in the same way. 

---

# LRAC Graph

The LRAC curve in the slides is **saucer-shaped**.

The three important regions are:

| LRAC behaviour | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Falling LRAC | Economies of scale |
| Flat/minimum region | Constant returns |
| Rising LRAC | Diseconomies of scale |

---

# Economies of Scale

When expanding production causes long-run average cost to fall:

\[
\boxed{Q\uparrow\Rightarrow LRAC\downarrow}
\]

This is:

\[
\boxed{\text{Economies of Scale}}
\]

The slides associate this region with **increasing returns**: output increases more than proportionally to inputs. 

### Increasing returns

If inputs rise by 10% and output rises by **more than 10%**:

\[
\boxed{\text{Increasing returns}}
\]

---

# Minimum Efficient Scale

The **minimum efficient scale (MES)** is:

\[
\boxed{\text{smallest output at which LRAC reaches its minimum}}
\]

At this point, all available economies of scale have been realized. 

This is different from Chapter 7's **capacity**:

- **Capacity** → minimum **short-run ATC**
- **MES** → smallest output at minimum **LRAC**

That distinction is worth putting on your cheat sheet.

---

# Constant Returns

Constant returns means:

\[
\boxed{\%\Delta Output=\%\Delta Inputs}
\]

If all inputs rise 10%, output rises 10%.

---

# Diseconomies of Scale / Decreasing Returns

Past the low-cost range, LRAC begins rising.

\[
\boxed{Q\uparrow\Rightarrow LRAC\uparrow}
\]

This is:

\[
\boxed{\text{Diseconomies of Scale}}
\]

The slides connect this with **decreasing returns**:

\[
\boxed{\%\Delta Output<\%\Delta Inputs}
\]

---

# LRAC vs. SRATC

This is probably one of the most important Chapter 8 graph topics.

### SRATC

Short-run average total cost:

\[
\boxed{\text{Some factors are fixed}}
\]

### LRAC

Long-run average cost:

\[
\boxed{\text{All factors are variable}}
\]

Therefore LRAC shows the **lowest attainable cost** for each output level. 

This means:

\[
\boxed{SRATC\text{ cannot lie below LRAC}}
\]

Each SRATC curve represents a different fixed plant size.

The SRATC touches the LRAC where that particular plant size is optimal:

\[
\boxed{\text{SRATC tangent to LRAC at its optimal output}}
\]

At other outputs:

\[
\boxed{SRATC>LRAC}
\]

### Exam method

If a question gives several plant-size SRATC curves:

1. Find the quantity being produced.
2. Look vertically at all available SRATC curves.
3. The lowest-cost plant at that quantity is the one closest to/touching LRAC.
4. If the firm is on a higher SRATC, it could lower cost by changing plant size.

---

# 8.2 Very Long Run: Technology

In the **very long run**, technology can change.

Technological changes shift the LRAC curve. 

### Productivity

Productivity means:

\[
\boxed{\text{Output produced per unit of input}}
\]

Examples:

\[
\boxed{\frac{\text{Output}}{\text{Worker}}}
\]

or:

\[
\boxed{\frac{\text{Output}}{\text{Hour worked}}}
\]

---

# Types of Technological Change

The slides identify three:

1. **New techniques**
2. **Improved inputs**
3. **New products**

Technology is described as **endogenous** because firms deliberately innovate in search of profits. 

---

# Substitute Away vs. Innovate Away

If an input becomes more expensive, firms can:

\[
\boxed{\text{Substitute away from it}}
\]

or:

\[
\boxed{\text{Innovate away from it}}
\]

or do both over different time horizons. 

Example:

Labour gets expensive.

**Long run:**

Use more machines and less labour.

**Very long run:**

Invent an entirely new automated technology.

---
